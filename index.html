What you are seeing

A head seen from above, about 1600 guard hairs rooted on its skin. Each hair is a small elastic rod that leans back toward the tail at the lie angle. Air flows around the head, slows down inside the coat, and pushes on each hair with ordinary cylinder drag. Nothing is scripted: the fur state comes from the force balance on every hair, about 240 times per simulated second.

Wind from the front runs along the grain. The drag on a backward-leaning hair then points into the skin, so the coat is pressed flat and stays inside its own sheltered layer. Wind from behind runs against the grain. The same drag now points out of the skin, lifts the hair tip into faster air, which lifts it further. This is a runaway: past a threshold the hair flips over and points forward, and the long crown and cheek hairs land across the eyes. The face itself sits in the separated wake, where it gets buffeted rather than combed.

With Let it turn on its own, the animal knows nothing about the wind direction. It only feels how disturbed its fur is. It keeps turning while that feeling gets better and reverses when it gets worse, a run-and-tumble search. It ends up nose into the wind because that is where the disturbance is lowest. That behaviour rule is an illustration, not a model of any animal's nervous system.

Forces and equations

1. Air at the skin. The head is treated as a sphere for the outer flow. Potential flow over a sphere gives the slip velocity on the surface, with n the outward skin normal and U the free-stream wind:

u_s = 1.5 · (U − (U·n) n) attached, β < β_sep u_s = −0.2 · (U − (U·n) n) separated wake, β > β_sep (reverse flow, weak) β = angle between n and the upstream direction; blended over ±12° around β_sep

Turbulence is added as a moving, spatially smooth random field of intensity TI in attached flow and 0.4 in the wake, with eddies about 6 cm across, advected with the wind, plus slow gusts of 0.6·TI on the free stream.

2. Shelter inside the coat. Inside the fur layer, speed decays exponentially with depth, the usual form for flow inside a plant or fur canopy. The coat top is the height of a hair lying at its rest angle.

u(z) = u_s · exp(−a · (1 − z/h_c)) for z < h_c, u(z) = u_s above h_c = L · sin θ0, z = 0.7 · L · (h·n) (height of the hair's load centre)

3. Drag on one hair. Only the part of the relative wind normal to the hair pushes on it (cross-flow on a cylinder):

w = u(z) − 0.7 · v_tip relative air velocity at the load centre w_n = w − (w·h) h f = ½ · ρ · C_D · d · |w_n| · w_n force per unit length C_D = 1 + 10 · Re^(−2/3), Re = |w_n| · d / ν (White's cylinder fit, 1 < Re < 2·10⁵) F_tip = f · L / 2 tip force with the same root moment as the uniform load

4. Hair stiffness. Each hair is a rigid rod on a root spring, sized so that it has the same root moment and tip deflection as a clamped cantilever under a uniform load:

I = π d⁴ / 64, k_θ = 4 E I / L, k_tip = k_θ / L² F_spring = k_tip · L · α · ê α = angle from rest direction, ê points back to rest m_eff = ρ_k · (π d²/4) · L / 3 (rod pivoting about its root) damping: internal ζ = 0.05 plus the aerodynamic damping already in w

5. Contact. A hair cannot go below θ_min = 4° off the skin (it lies on the undercoat). The tip is integrated with a linearised backward Euler step, which is stable for the stiff, millisecond-scale short hairs on the snout:

v' = (m v + Δt (F_spring + F_wind)) / (m + Δt (0.7 C + c_int) + Δt² k_tip)

Ruffled means a hair is more than 40° away from its rest direction. Across the eyes means a hair segment passes within one eye radius of an eye centre while pointing forward.

Sanity numbers, red fox defaults (L = 35 mm, d = 75 µm): the root stiffness is k_θ ≈ 6·10⁻⁷ N·m/rad and a crown hair's natural frequency is about 14 Hz. With 9 m/s from behind, the drag on a resting hair already lifts it about 20° by hand calculation, which exposes it to faster air, and it flips. With the same wind from the front the drag points into the skin.

Two things fall out. Short, fine coats tolerate a light tailwind: the cat's coat only starts to flip at about 4 m/s, the fox's at about 2 to 3 m/s. And very long coats get tousled even facing the wind, because the back of the head sits in the reverse flow of the wake; facing the wind is still far better, but not perfect.

What this leaves out
  • The body and neck. A real animal's body sits behind the head and shelters it from a tailwind, and ears catch wind on their own.
  • Hair-to-hair friction and the way neighbouring hairs hold each other down. The shelter term stands in for this.
  • Bending along the hair. Each hair is a rigid rod on a spring. It is drawn curved from its root for looks, and its curve is not computed.
  • Rain, cold and eyes. Fur is only one reason to face the wind. Horses and cattle often stand rump-to-wind in cold rain, which protects the face and eyes and puts the thickest coat to the weather. Bison and many birds face into storms; for birds the grain argument is the same, with feathers instead of hair.
